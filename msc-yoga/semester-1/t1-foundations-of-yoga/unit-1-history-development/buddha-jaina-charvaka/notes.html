In one paragraph

Buddhism, Jainism and Charvaka are the nastika (heterodox) streams: they do not accept the authority of the Vedas. They differ sharply among themselves. Buddhism says there is no permanent self and teaches the middle path to nirvana. Jainism says there are many eternal souls bound by karmic matter and teaches ahimsa and self-effort. Charvaka accepts only perception and treats consciousness as a product of matter. BJC notes

1AAstika and nastika

GroupMeaningSchools
Astika (orthodox)Accept the authority of the VedasNyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Purva Mimamsa, Vedanta
Nastika (heterodox)Do not accept the authority of the VedasBuddhism, Jainism, Charvaka (Lokayata)
Know this
  • Nastika here means rejecting Vedic authority; it does not simply mean atheist. BJC notes
  • Buddhism and Jainism belong to the shramana (ascetic, renunciant) tradition and arose in the 6th to 5th century BCE in the Gangetic plains, rejecting animal sacrifice and ritualism and stressing ethics, self-effort and liberation from suffering. BJC notes
  • Charvaka is materialist and sceptical: it rejects the Vedas, the soul, karma, rebirth and liberation beyond death. BJC notes

02 · Buddhism

Gautama Buddha: Four Truths, Eightfold Path, No-Self

Life, teaching, metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, liberation and schools.

2AThe Buddha and the Four Noble Truths

Know this
  • Siddhartha Gautama, born at Lumbini in the Shakya clan, 6th to 5th century BCE (traditional dating; modern scholars differ by a few decades). BJC notes
  • Renounced after seeing old age, sickness and death; practised extreme austerity, then took the Middle Path. Enlightenment (bodhi) at Bodh Gaya; first sermon (Dhammachakkappavattana) at Sarnath; mahaparinirvana at Kushinagar. BJC notes
  • Teachings are in the Tripitaka (Pali canon): Vinaya, Sutta, Abhidhamma. BJC notes
Noble TruthMeaning
1. DukkhaLife involves suffering and unsatisfactoriness: birth, ageing, illness, death, separation, unfulfilled desire, instability of conditioned things
2. SamudayaCause: craving (tanha) rooted in ignorance (avidya)
3. NirodhaCessation: nirvana, the ending of craving
4. MaggaThe path: the Noble Eightfold Path

Eightfold path = Sila (conduct) + Samadhi (meditation) + Prajna (wisdom). BJC notes

2CMetaphysics: three marks and dependent origination

DoctrineMeaning
AniccaImpermanence: all conditioned things change (momentariness, kshanikavada, in later schools)
DukkhaClinging to impermanent things leads to suffering
AnattaNo permanent soul; the person is a stream of five skandhas: form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness
PratityasamutpadaEverything arises depending on conditions: “when this exists, that comes to be”

Twelve nidanas: avidya, samskara, vijnana, namarupa, shadayatana, sparsha, vedana, trishna, upadana, bhava, jati, jaramarana. BJC notes

Know this
  • Karma and rebirth: intentional action (cetana) shapes future experience; rebirth is the continuity of a causal stream with no permanent self passing on. BJC notes
  • No creator God is posited; the focus is causal law (dhamma). BJC notes
  • Avyakata: the Buddha declined speculative questions (is the world eternal?) because they do not end suffering. BJC notes

2DEpistemology

Know this
  • Ehipassiko, “come and see”: test by experience. The Kalama Sutta advises not to accept something merely from tradition, authority or hearsay. BJC notes
  • Perception and inference are valid, formalised by later logicians Dignaga and Dharmakirti. BJC notes
  • Two truths: conventional (samvriti) and ultimate (paramartha), developed in Madhyamaka. Knowledge is judged by whether it reduces craving, hatred and delusion. BJC notes

2EEthics and liberation

SetContents
Five precepts (pancha sila)Abstain from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, intoxicants that cloud the mind
BrahmaviharasMetta (loving-kindness), karuna (compassion), mudita (sympathetic joy), upekkha (equanimity)
Paramitas (Mahayana)Generosity, ethical conduct, patience, effort, meditation, wisdom (and others)
Know this
  • Ethics rests on non-harm and compassion; intention largely decides the moral quality of an act. BJC notes
  • Nirvana (nibbana): extinguishing the three fires of greed, hatred and delusion, ending samsara. Realised by wisdom born of ethics and meditation, not ritual or grace. BJC notes
  • Arhat (Theravada ideal) has attained nirvana; Bodhisattva (Mahayana ideal) delays final liberation to help all beings. BJC notes

03 · Jainism

Mahavira: Souls, Karma and Ahimsa

Teachers, the soul and non-soul, karma as matter, many-sided logic and moksha.

3AOrigin and teachers

Know this
  • 24 Tirthankaras (“ford-makers”): Rishabhanatha (first), Parshvanatha (23rd, historically recognised), Mahavira (24th). BJC notes
  • Vardhamana Mahavira: born about the 6th century BCE at Kundagrama near Vaishali (dates vary); renounced at about thirty, practised severe austerity for about twelve years, attained kevala jnana (omniscience), taught for about thirty years, liberated at Pavapuri. BJC notes
  • Sects: Digambara (sky-clad monks; hold women cannot attain liberation directly in that birth) and Shvetambara (white-clad). Scriptures: the Agamas. BJC notes

3BMetaphysics: realism and pluralism

Know this
  • Reality is eternal in substance and changing in modes. Basic categories: jiva (soul) and ajiva (non-soul). BJC notes
  • Jiva: conscious, eternal, with infinite potential for knowledge, perception, bliss and energy (ananta chatushtaya) obscured by karma; many souls, each taking the size of its body (dehapramana); classified by number of senses (one to five). BJC notes
  • Ajiva has five substances: pudgala (matter), dharma (medium of motion), adharma (medium of rest), akasha (space), kala (time). With jiva: six dravyas. BJC notes
  • No creator God; the universe is beginningless and eternal, governed by natural law and karma. BJC notes
Seven tattvasMeaning
JivaSoul
AjivaNon-soul substances
AsravaInflux of karma into the soul
BandhaBondage by karma
SamvaraStopping the influx
NirjaraShedding accumulated karma
MokshaLiberation

Some lists add punya (merit) and papa (demerit), making nine. BJC notes

Karma in Jainism
  • Karma is a subtle material substance that sticks to the soul through the activity of body, speech and mind combined with passions (kashayas: anger, pride, deceit, greed). BJC notes
  • Eight types: four ghatiya that obscure the soul’s qualities (jnanavaraniya, darshanavaraniya, mohaniya, antaraya) and four aghatiya (vedaniya, ayu, nama, gotra). BJC notes

3CEpistemology

ConceptMeaning
Five kinds of knowledgeMati (sensory), shruta (scriptural), avadhi (clairvoyance), manahparyaya (telepathy), kevala (omniscience)
PramanasPratyaksha (direct) and paroksha (indirect: memory, recognition, inference, testimony)
AnekantavadaReality has many aspects; no single view captures it
SyadvadaConditional predication with “syat” (“in some respect”); seven-fold (saptabhangi)
NayavadaPartial standpoints; each valid only from its perspective

The parable of the blind men and the elephant illustrates anekantavada; the doctrine promotes tolerance and non-dogmatism. BJC notes

3DEthics and liberation

Know this
  • Ahimsa paramo dharmah: non-violence is the highest duty, in thought, speech and action, towards all living beings including micro-organisms and plants. BJC notes
  • Five great vows (Mahavratas) for ascetics: ahimsa, satya, asteya, brahmacharya, aparigraha. Laypeople follow smaller anuvratas. BJC notes
  • Triratna: samyak darshana (right faith or perception), samyak jnana (right knowledge), samyak charitra (right conduct): together the path to liberation. BJC notes
  • Tapas: twelve types, external (fasting, reduced diet) and internal (penance, humility, service, study, meditation, abandoning attachment). Sallekhana: voluntary fasting at life’s end under strict conditions. BJC notes
  • Moksha: complete shedding of all karma, so the soul realises infinite knowledge, perception, bliss, energy. The siddha rises to the top of the universe (Siddhashila) and abides eternally. BJC notes
  • By self-effort: no God grants liberation. Path: stop new karma (samvara), shed old karma (nirjara). BJC notes

Link to yoga: the five mahavratas closely match Patanjali’s yamas; kayotsarga and preksha dhyana; non-violence towards the body in asana links to ahimsa and anekanta. BJC notes

04 · Charvaka

Lokayata: Perception, Matter and Pleasure

The materialist school: what it accepts, what it denies, and why we know it only through critics.

4AIntroduction and sources

Know this
  • Also Lokayata (“prevalent among the people”) or Barhaspatya (after Brihaspati): the materialist, naturalist school. BJC notes
  • Original works are lost. Its views are known from opponents’ summaries, e.g. Madhavacharya’s Sarva-Darshana-Sangraha, so opponents may have presented them in their own light. BJC notes
  • A related sceptical text, Tattvopaplavasimha of Jayarasi Bhatta, questions even the means of knowledge. BJC notes

4BEpistemology

Know this
  • Only perception (pratyaksha) is valid. BJC notes
  • Inference rejected: it needs invariable concomitance (vyapti), which can never be established with certainty since not all instances can be observed; at best it gives probability. BJC notes
  • Testimony rejected: the Vedas are the work of fallible people, often serving priestly interests. So claims about God, soul, heaven, hell and karma are unfounded. BJC notes
Criticism

Opponents argue that rejecting inference is self-defeating: doubting inference, or arguing for a position, seems to use it; and daily life, such as predicting the sunrise, relies on it. BJC notes

4CMetaphysics, ethics, liberation

Know this
  • Four elements only: earth, water, fire, air. Akasha is not accepted, because it is not perceived. BJC notes
  • Consciousness arises from the body, as an intoxicating power arises when ingredients are fermented together (the kinva analogy). At death consciousness ends. No separate soul; the self is the body qualified by consciousness. BJC notes
  • No God, karma, rebirth, heaven or hell; the world arises by svabhava (nature). BJC notes
  • Ethics: pleasure (sukha) is the aim. Of the four purusharthas only artha and kama are accepted; dharma (as ritual duty) and moksha are rejected. Pleasure mixed with pain is not rejected, just as one does not stop eating fish because of the bones. Vedic ritual is condemned as unproductive and exploitative. BJC notes
  • Liberation: none beyond this life; “death alone is liberation”; freedom from pain is only worldly (release from sickness or poverty). BJC notes

Often labelled crude hedonism; some scholars note a more reflective version that avoids harm and stresses present, worldly happiness. Significance: the sceptical, rationalist strand; it forced other schools to refine their theories of inference and to defend the soul and rebirth. BJC notes

10 marksNastika

Explain the thoughts of Buddha, Jaina and Charvaka.

Tap to reveal answer

Introduction. Nastika schools reject Vedic authority. Buddhism and Jainism are shramana traditions of the 6th to 5th century BCE; Charvaka is materialist.

Buddhism. Four Noble Truths and Eightfold Path (sila, samadhi, prajna); anicca, dukkha, anatta; dependent origination and twelve nidanas; knowledge tested by experience (Kalama Sutta); five precepts and brahmaviharas; nirvana as extinction of greed, hatred and delusion.

Jainism. Mahavira, 24th Tirthankara; jiva and ajiva, seven tattvas, karma as subtle matter; anekantavada, syadvada, nayavada; five mahavratas, ahimsa highest; triratna; moksha by self-effort.

Charvaka. Only perception; four elements; consciousness from matter; pleasure the aim; no soul, rebirth or liberation beyond death.

Conclusion. All three reject the Vedas but differ on soul, karma and God; together they challenged ritualism and sharpened debates on knowledge and ethics.

5 marksBJC

Explain the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path.

Tap to reveal answer

Truths: dukkha (suffering), samudaya (craving rooted in ignorance), nirodha (cessation, nirvana), magga (the path). The path groups into wisdom (right view, intention), conduct (right speech, action, livelihood) and meditation (right effort, mindfulness, concentration).

5 marksBJC

Explain the Jain concept of Tri-ratna.

Tap to reveal answer

The three jewels, the path to liberation: samyak darshana (right faith or perception), samyak jnana (right knowledge), samyak charitra (right conduct, the five great vows). With samvara and nirjara they stop and shed karma.

5 marksBJC

Explain anekantavada, syadvada and nayavada.

Tap to reveal answer

Anekantavada: reality has many aspects and no single view is complete (blind men and elephant). Syadvada: statements are qualified by “syat”, seven-fold predication. Nayavada: partial standpoints, each valid from its own perspective. They promote tolerance.

5 marksBJC

Explain the Charvaka theory of knowledge and its criticism.

Tap to reveal answer

Only perception is valid; inference fails because vyapti cannot be established with certainty; testimony, including the Vedas, is unreliable. Critics say rejecting inference is self-defeating and everyday life relies on it.

5 marksBJC

Explain dependent origination.

Tap to reveal answer

Pratityasamutpada: everything arises depending on conditions. Twelve links from avidya to old age and death: avidya, samskara, vijnana, namarupa, shadayatana, sparsha, vedana, trishna, upadana, bhava, jati, jaramarana. It explains suffering and rebirth without a permanent self.
