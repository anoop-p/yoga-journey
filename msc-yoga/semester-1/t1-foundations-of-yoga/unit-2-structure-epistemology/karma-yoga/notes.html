Explain Karma Yoga. (Study version, about 900 words; write the core, about 500, in the exam.)

1. Introduction

Karma means action, deed or activity, and yoga means union or spiritual discipline. Karma Yoga is the discipline of performing one’s duty without attachment to the fruits of action, offering the actions to the Divine and keeping equanimity in success and failure. It is strongly associated with the Bhagavad Gita.

The basic problem is not action but attachment to its result. The thought “I must succeed” creates attachment, then fear and anxiety. Success brings ego, and failure brings disappointment and mental disturbance. Karma Yoga changes the attitude: do the duty sincerely, give your best effort, do not cling to the result, offer it to Ishvara and stay balanced. It is not simply “doing good things”; it is a particular attitude towards action and its results.

2. Historical and Philosophical Background

Karma Yoga has its roots in the Vedic tradition, where action and ritual were important. Its most systematic presentation is in the Bhagavad Gita, in Krishna’s teaching to Arjuna, who faces a moral crisis about whether to act or withdraw from action. Krishna teaches duty, action, non-attachment, dharma, selfless action, knowledge, devotion and liberation. His message is not to stop acting but to change the way one acts.

3. Structure

Karma Yoga is a chain from action to liberation: Karma → Dharma/Svadharma → Nishkama karma → Ishvara-pranidhana → Samatva → Chitta-shuddhi → Jnana → Moksha.

Svadharma is one’s own appropriate duty according to one’s situation. A student’s duty is to study, a teacher’s to teach, a parent’s to care for the family and a professional’s to work sincerely. It should not be reduced to doing whatever job society assigns, because it sits within the Gita’s wider ethical and philosophical frame. Samatva is equanimity, and chitta-shuddhi is the purification of the mind, which prepares it for knowledge.

Karma is traditionally of three types: sanchita (the accumulated store of consequences from past actions), prarabdha (the portion already bearing fruit, associated with this life) and agami or kriyamana (karma generated by present action, whose effects may come later). These are traditional doctrinal classifications, not scientifically established mechanisms.

4. Method

Sakama karma is action motivated by the desire for a specific result, such as money, praise or status. Nishkama karma is action performed without attachment to the fruit: “I perform my duty sincerely, without making my happiness depend on the outcome.” Nishkama does not mean action without intention; it means freedom from selfish attachment to the fruits.

The Gita gives four key sentences:

  • Gita 2.47, karmany evadhikaras te ma phaleshu kadachana: you have a right to action but not to its fruits. This does not mean “do not care about the result”; your responsibility is to perform the action properly, because you cannot fully control the outcome.
  • Gita 2.50, yogah karmasu kaushalam: yoga is skill in action, meaning action done with wisdom, balance and the right attitude, not mere technical skill.
  • Gita 2.48, samatvam yoga ucyate: equanimity is called yoga.
  • Gita 3.9: action not done in a spirit of offering causes bondage.

Samatva means that in success one does not become proud and in failure one does not break down: one does the action, accepts the result with balance, learns and continues. Yajna was originally a sacrificial ritual, but the Gita broadens it to action done in a spirit of offering and duty. The thought “I do everything for myself” becomes “my action is an offering”, and ordinary work becomes spiritual practice.

Two attitudes support this. Ishvara-arpita buddhi is offering one’s actions to Ishvara. Prasada buddhi is receiving the result as prasada, accepted with equanimity and not judged against personal expectations.

Practical exercise from the notes: take a task you do not enjoy and ask three questions. Before Karma Yoga: “What will I get from this?” With the attitude: “Can I do this sincerely as my responsibility, without constant worry about reward?” Result: “Can I accept the outcome without losing mental balance?”

Flowchart (core: remember this)

Human being has to act→Dharma / duty (svadharma)→Perform action sincerely→Nishkama attitude (no attachment to fruits)→Offer action to Ishvara (ishvara-arpita buddhi)→Accept result as prasada (prasada buddhi)→Samatva (equanimity)→Chitta-shuddhi (purification of mind)→Jnana (knowledge)→Moksha (liberation)

5. Epistemology

Epistemology is how valid knowledge is obtained. Karma Yoga is primarily an ethical and practical path, not an independent epistemological system, and it is understood within the Gita and Vedantic framework. Knowledge comes through the standard pramanas: pratyaksha (perception), anumana (inference) and shabda (testimony or scripture). Its special contribution is that right action purifies the mind and prepares the seeker for higher knowledge, called chitta-shuddhi. The sequence is: less selfish attachment, chitta-shuddhi, greater clarity, the capacity for jnana and then liberation.

6. Metaphysics

The key concepts are Atman (the deeper Self), Prakriti (nature or material manifestation), Ishvara (the Lord, in the Gita’s theology), karma (action and its consequences), dharma (cosmic and ethical order and duty) and moksha (liberation). The Gita presents human beings as acting within Prakriti and experiencing the consequences of their actions; through yoga they transform their relationship to action.

Action itself does not necessarily bind. Attachment to action and its fruits does. The cycle of bondage is: desire → action → attachment to the result → success or failure → further desire → bondage. Karma Yoga breaks it: dharma → action → non-attachment → purification → liberation.

Connections and conclusion

Karma Yoga supports and prepares the ground for the other paths. Selfless action purifies the mind and makes it ready for jnana. Action offered to Ishvara and the result taken as prasada develops bhakti. Reduced mental disturbance helps concentration in raja yoga. Karma Yoga does not teach us to stop acting. It teaches us to transform the way we act, performing our duty without selfish attachment to its fruits.
