01 · Big picture

Levels of Organisation: From Cell to Organism

Cells that look alike and work together form a tissue; tissues build organs; organs team up as systems. The four basic tissues are the bricks of every organ.

  1. Cell – the smallest living unit.
  2. Tissue – many similar cells doing one job.Tissues Detailed Notes
  3. Organ – several different tissues working as one structure (stomach, liver, intestine, pancreas).
  4. Organ system – many organs on one big task (digestive: mouth, food pipe, stomach, intestine, liver).
  5. Organism – all the systems together.
Understand it

Your notes explain a tissue with a wedding in the house. The father splits the work: four cousins who love food arrange the meals, four strong cousins carry and serve, four good talkers look after the guests. Each group is similar in ability and does one job. That is exactly a tissue: the body hands different jobs to different groups of cells.Tissues Detailed Notes

The two key words in the definition are similar in structure (they look alike and lie together) and work together (on one assigned job). Your quick notes add a third: similar in genesis, meaning they come from the same origin in the embryo.Tissues Quick Notes

1AWhy a many-celled body needs tissues

Know this · three points (NCERT question)
  • Division of labour: different groups of cells do different jobs, so work is efficient and does not get muddled.
  • Structural support: tissues such as bone give the framework that lets the body stand upright.
  • Efficient functioning: many cells doing one job together do it faster and better than one cell alone.
Memory aid

“Divide, Support, Team up.” Three uses of tissues.

Cell → Tissue → Organ → System → Organism: “Can Tigers Observe Silent Owls?”

02 · Staying steady

Homeostasis & Feedback

Every tissue works only within a narrow range of temperature, water, sugar and pH. Homeostasis is the body noticing a drift and pulling itself back.

  1. Stimulus: something pushes a variable away from its normal value.
  2. Receptor: a sensor detects the change.
  3. Control centre: compares it with the set point and decides. The hypothalamus regulates homeostasis in many cases.Yoga & Human Body
  4. Effector: the muscle or gland that acts.
  5. Response: the action; in negative feedback it cancels the original change.
Understand it

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment despite changes outside the body.Cell Study Notes; Yoga & Human Body It is not a fixed number; it is a narrow range. Healthy body temperature is about 37 °C (98.6 °F), and within about a degree is still normal. If it drifts too far, crucial molecules change shape and stop working. That is why the body fights even small drifts.Cell Detailed Notes

Think of a thermostat at home: the room cools, the sensor notices, the heater switches on, the room warms, the heater switches off. The body does the same thing with many variables at once: temperature, blood glucose, blood pressure, blood pH, fluid balance, calcium level, oxygen and carbon dioxide levels.Cell Study Notes; Yoga & Human Body

Why it matters to a cell: a cell works properly only when its surroundings stay within limits, and the cell membrane helps by controlling water, ions, nutrients and waste. Chain: membrane → transport → internal environment → homeostasis. (See the Cell page.)Cell Study Notes

03 · The covering tissue

Epithelial Tissue

Cells packed edge to edge like tiles, lining every surface and cavity. The names tell you the layers and the shape; the shape tells you the job.

WhereWhat that organ doesWhat the epithelium does there
SkinProtects the bodyProtection
Inner lining of the intestineAbsorbs digested food into the bloodAbsorption
Tubules of the kidneyFiltrationFiltration
Liver and glandsMake a juiceSecretion
Alveoli of the lungsTake O₂ in, send CO₂ outExchange of gases

Tissues Detailed Notes, 3.1

Understand it

Epithelium is the protective covering tissue of animals, found in almost all organs, and it does a different job in different places.Tissues Detailed Notes Its three general features explain this:

  • No intercellular space. The cells are very tightly packed, so nothing leaks between them; materials must pass through the cells. That is what makes it a good barrier.
  • A cementing material sticks the cells together, the way a mason uses cement to join bricks (your quick notes name it as mucoprotein: hyaluronic acid with calcium salts).Tissues Detailed + Quick Notes
  • A basement membrane separates it from the tissue below. It is non-cellular (it has no cells).Tissues Detailed Notes

Because epithelium has no blood vessels of its own in most places, it takes food from the connective tissue below, which is why that connective layer sits so close.general histology

Two layers under the epithelium. The detailed notes say the epithelium sits on a non-cellular basement membrane. The quick notes say it rests on the lamina propria, a loose vascular connective tissue. Both are right: the basement membrane is the thin non-cellular sheet directly under the cells, and the lamina propria is the connective layer beneath that. For the exam write the basement membrane (and name the lamina propria if the question says “membrane”).

3BTypes of epithelium, side by side

  1. Simple squamous: one layer of thin, flat cells. Blood vessels, alveoli, serous membranes (pleura, peritoneum), nephron, heart lining. Very thin so O₂ and food pass out and CO₂ comes in: transport, exchange, filtration.
  2. Simple cuboidal: one layer of cubes. Kidney tubules, salivary-gland ducts, thyroid. Filtration, secretion, support. Asked often.
  3. Simple columnar: tall pillars. Stomach, intestine, gland ducts; goblet cells make mucus. Absorption and secretion.
  4. Ciliated columnar: columnar with cilia. Trachea and respiratory tract, fallopian tube. Cilia push mucus (with trapped dust) onward so it is cleared.
  5. Stratified squamous: many layers, flat on top. Skin, mouth, pharynx, oesophagus. Protection against rubbing, e.g. sharp food in the mouth. Asked often.
  6. Transitional: stretchable, with rounded surface cells. Renal pelvis, ureter, bladder, urethra. Lets the organ stretch and shrink.

Explain it to someone · 30 seconds

“Epithelium is the body’s wall-paper and wrapping. Where it needs to be a thin window, like the lung air sacs, it is one flat layer. Where it takes wear, like skin, it is many layers. Go deeper · why shape equals job

Flat cells are thin, so gases and liquids cross in a short distance (alveoli, vessel lining). Cube cells have more cytoplasm and organelles for pumping and secreting (kidney tubules, glands). Tall cells have room for many organelles and a large surface: absorbing and secreting (gut). Layers add protection from wear (skin, mouth). Stretchy layers allow filling (bladder).general histology

04 · The connecting and supporting tissue

Connective Tissue

Few cells scattered far apart in plenty of matrix. The matrix decides the job: jelly, fluid, flexible or rock-hard.

4AWhat makes a tissue “connective”

  1. Collagen (white) fibres: strong, non-branching bundles, made by fibroblasts. Resist pulling.
  2. Elastic (yellow) fibres: thin, wavy, made of elastin. Stretch and spring back.
  3. Fibroblast: the cell that makes the fibres and matrix.
  4. Macrophage (histiocyte): eats germs and debris.
  5. Mast cell: releases chemicals in inflammation (histamine, general physiology).
  6. Ground substance: the jelly-like matrix in which everything sits.
Understand it

Epithelium is a crowd packed shoulder to shoulder. Connective tissue is the opposite: the cells are loosely packed and lie far apart, with a lot of space between them, and that space is filled with a matrix that holds the cells together. The matrix can be jelly-like, fluid or rigid.Tissues Detailed Notes

Chikki analogy: the peanuts lie far apart and are stuck together by jaggery. Peanuts = cells, jaggery = matrix.Tissues Detailed Notes Your quick notes also call connective tissue mesenchymal tissue (it develops from the mesoderm), with few cells and abundant matrix, the opposite of epithelium.Connective Tissue Notes

Functions: protection, support, transport, storing fat, filling spaces, connecting tissues and giving flexibility.Tissues Detailed Notes

4FTendons and ligaments

Points to verify (from your notes).
  • Areolar and repair. The lecture says areolar cells “become skin” after injury. Textbooks say areolar (loose connective) tissue supports and helps repair; the epidermis regenerates from its own deeper cells. Treat the lecture wording as a simplification.
  • “Most abundant tissue.” The lecture calls areolar tissue the most abundant tissue in animals. Many textbooks say it is the most widely distributed connective tissue. Check the wording for your exam.
  • Adipose is described in the quick notes as a “loose connective tissue containing fat”; adipocytes fill almost the whole tissue.
Common confusion
  • Tendon vs ligament: tendon = muscle to bone and non-elastic; ligament = bone to bone and elastic.
  • Bone vs cartilage: bone is hard (calcium phosphate); cartilage is flexible.
  • Blood is a tissue, not just a liquid: cells in a fluid matrix.
  • Bone cells vs bone matrix: the cells are few; most of bone is matrix.

Go deeper · what fibroblasts, osteoblasts and chondroblasts have in common

The “-blast” cells are the young, active builders of a tissue (fibroblast: fibres; chondroblast: cartilage; osteoblast: bone), and when they get walled in by their own matrix they become the resting “-cyte” cells (chondrocyte, osteocyte). That is why cartilage and bone cells are found in lacunae.general histology

Cartilage has no blood vessels, so it heals slowly; bone is rich in vessels, so it heals well.general histology

05 · The moving tissue

Muscular Tissue

Long cells packed with contractile proteins that shorten when told to. Three kinds: skeletal, smooth and cardiac.

Understand it

Muscles are specialised tissues built for movement: walking, writing, food moving down the oesophagus, the beating of the heart. Four facts explain everything:Tissues Detailed Notes

  • Muscles only pull. Contraction shortens the muscle and pulls in one direction. To move back the other way another muscle is needed, so skeletal muscles work in pairs: the triceps straightens the arm, the biceps bends it back.
  • Contractile proteins. Muscle cells are long fibre-like cells containing proteins that shorten and lengthen; shortening is contraction, returning is relaxation. Plants have none, so they do not show this movement.
  • Energy. Movement needs a lot, so muscle cells are packed with mitochondria.
  • Heat. Shivering is rapid contraction and relaxation, producing heat.

Quick notes: the two key properties are contractility (on excitation) and conductivity.Tissues Quick Notes

5BDraw a muscle cell (Q8, 2 marks)

  1. Sarcolemma: the cell membrane of a muscle fibre.
  2. Nuclei: many, flat, lying at the periphery just under the sarcolemma.
  3. Myofibrils with striations: thread-like bundles along the cell made of myosin (thick, about 100 Å) and actin (thin, about 50 Å) filaments. Their overlap gives the dark and light bands.
  4. Mitochondria (sarcosomes): many, for ATP.
  5. Sarcoplasm (not numbered): cytoplasm containing myoglobin (oxygen store), glycogen and the organelles.Tissues Quick Notes

  2. Perimysium: covers each bundle (fascicle).
  3. Fascicle: a bundle of muscle fibres.
  4. Endomysium: covers each single fibre.

Understand it · why the heart has its own muscle

The heart’s movement is involuntary, so you might expect smooth muscle. But the heartbeat is continuous: it starts at birth and does not stop until death. That needs a muscle that never takes a break, and that is cardiac muscle. Its intercalated discs join the cells so they contract together in a rhythm; the cells also form a 3-D network that can start its own beat (spontaneous rhythmicity).Tissues Detailed + Quick Notes

Why skeletal muscle moves bones: a signal from the brain reaches the muscle; the muscle is attached to bone by the tendon, so the bone moves with it.Tissues Detailed Notes

Common confusion
  • Muscles push? No, they only pull. Pushing comes from a partner muscle pulling the other way.
  • Voluntary vs striated. Two separate ideas. Cardiac is striated but involuntary.
  • Smooth muscle is not weak. It moves food and controls blood flow, just without conscious control.

06 · The signalling tissue

Nervous Tissue

Neurons: the longest cells in the body, built to carry messages quickly from one part to another. Supported by neuroglia.

Understand it

Nervous tissue is found in the brain, spinal cord and nerves. The “wires” that come out of the brain and spinal cord and carry signals around the body are nerves, and they are made of nervous tissue.Tissues Detailed Notes It is an excitable tissue that receives and transmits impulses, made of neurons (the cells that signal) and neuroglia (the cells that support and insulate).Tissues Quick Notes

Neurons are the longest cells in the body, up to about a metre. The job: carry messages from the brain and spinal cord to the parts of the body, and from the body back to the brain. When you hold a cup of tea, a signal travels along nerves to say “hot”.Tissues Detailed Notes

Three kinds of “matter” in the nervous system: grey matter (nerve cells), white matter (nerve fibres) and neuroglia (support). Nervous tissue comes from the embryonic ectoderm.Tissues Quick Notes

6AThe neuron

  1. Dendrites: short, branched, thread-like projections that catch the signal and carry it towards the cell body.
  2. Cell body (cyton, soma, perikaryon): the round part with the nucleus (control centre). Its cytoplasm (neuroplasm) holds Nissl bodies (rough ER and ribosomes for making proteins), mitochondria, Golgi and neurofibrils.
  3. Axon: the single long, fibre-like tail that carries the impulse away from the cell body.
  4. Myelin sheath: fatty insulating wrapping on many axons; makes conduction faster.
  5. Node of Ranvier: the small gaps between myelin segments.
  6. Nerve endings: the root-like ends of the axon, where the impulse passes to the next cell.

Tissues Detailed + Quick Notes Myelin extras: myelin is present in white matter and absent in grey matter; the neurolemma covers peripheral nerve fibres (absent on myelinated fibres in the brain and spinal cord).

Myelin: a scope note. Your lecture shows the myelin sheath in yellow and says it is not in the school syllabus, so leave it for now; your quick notes include it (with nodes of Ranvier and neurolemma). For M.Sc. include it, but know it is the “extra” part. Also, “up to one metre” is an approximation; some neurons are longer in tall people.
Memory aid

Neuron, in order: Dendrites → Cell body → Axon → Nerve ending. (“Dogs Catch All Noise.”)Tissues Detailed Notes

Dendrites carry to the body, axons carry away. (“A for Away.”)

6BStimulus, impulse and the path of a signal

  1. Axon terminal of the first neuron.
  2. Vesicles store the chemical messenger (neurotransmitter). They fuse with the membrane by exocytosis and release it.Cell Detailed Notes
  3. Synapse: the tiny gap crossed by the chemical.
  4. Receptors on the next neuron (lock and key) pick up the message and start a new impulse.
Know this · path of an impulse (the hot-tea example)

Dendrite of the first neuron (receives “hot”) → cell body → axon → nerve ending → across the synapse → dendrite of the next neuron → its cell body, axon and nerve ending … until the message reaches the brain, all within microseconds.Tissues Detailed Notes

  • A stimulus is any change in surroundings (feeling cold). An impulse is the signal (sometimes called an electric impulse because it passes along a nerve like current along a wire).
  • Functions: moving muscles; letting animals respond quickly to a stimulus.

6CThe resting potential and the action potential

  1. Resting potential: at rest the inside of a neuron is negative compared with the outside, about -70 mV. It depends on ion gradients, membrane permeability, the Na⁺/K⁺ pump and K⁺ leak channels.Yoga & Human Body
  2. Depolarisation: the membrane suddenly turns less negative and then positive inside (Na⁺ enters).general physiology
  3. Repolarisation: the inside returns toward negative (K⁺ leaves).general physiology
  4. Hyperpolarisation: a brief overshoot below the resting value before settling back.

The four-step order is from your Yoga & Human Body notes; which ions cross at each step is textbook physiology (see the Cell page for the Na⁺/K⁺ pump).

7CModel answer: “Explain how asanas and pranayama are good for muscles and bones” (Q22, 10 marks)

Plan (5 parts, about 2 marks each)

(1) Muscle (stretch, strengthen, reciprocal inhibition, isometric holds) → (2) Bone and joints (loading, tendons and ligaments stabilise, cartilage cushions) → (3) Nervous control (proprioception, balance, vagal relaxation) → (4) Pranayama (breathing, oxygen supply, relaxation of muscle tension) → (5) Conclusion and caution (may, depends on technique and individual).

About 220 words you can adapt

Asanas act on muscle, tendon, ligament, cartilage and bone. Stretching postures lengthen muscle and connective tissue and may improve flexibility; strengthening postures raise muscle force, and holding a posture is an isometric contraction. In stretching, reciprocal inhibition lets the opposite muscle relax. Tendons transfer muscle force to bone, and ligaments stabilise joints and prevent excessive movement, so postures should be built up gradually. “Pressing” postures load body tissues, including bone, so regular, appropriate practice may support bone and joint health; joint mobility may improve.

Balance postures train proprioception (muscle spindles, Golgi tendon organs and joint receptors), improving postural control. Slow, comfortable breathing in pranayama may increase vagal (parasympathetic) influence and helps release muscular tension, and good breathing supports the oxygen that muscle mitochondria need.

The effect depends on the technique, duration and the individual; claims should be separated from established physiology, and practice should be guided to avoid injury.
