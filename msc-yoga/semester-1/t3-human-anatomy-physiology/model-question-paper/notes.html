U1 Q1 · 2 marksCell

Define a cell. State the cell theory.

Tap to reveal answer
The cell is the smallest structural and functional unit of life.

Cell theory: all living things are made of cells; the cell is the basic unit; new cells come only from existing cells.

general

U1 Q2 · 2 marksCell

Who discovered the cell, and how?

Tap to reveal answer
Robert Hooke, in 1665.

He looked at a thin slice of cork under a simple microscope and saw box-like compartments, which he called cells. Leeuwenhoek later saw living cells.

general

U1 Q3 · 2 marksCell

What is the difference between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells?

Tap to reveal answer
A eukaryotic cell has a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; a prokaryotic cell has neither.

Human cells are eukaryotic; bacteria are prokaryotic.

general

U1 Q4 · 2 marksMembrane

What is the fluid mosaic model?

Tap to reveal answer
The plasma membrane is a flexible double layer of phospholipids with proteins floating in it like tiles in a mosaic.

Proposed by Singer and Nicolson (1972). Cholesterol keeps it stable; carbohydrates on the outside act as ID tags.

general

U1 Q5 · 2 marksOrganelles

Name two organelles with a double membrane.

Tap to reveal answer
The nucleus and the mitochondrion.

The nuclear envelope has two layers with pores; the mitochondrion has an outer and a folded inner membrane (cristae).

general

U1 Q6 · 2 marksOrganelles

What is the function of ribosomes?

Tap to reveal answer
Ribosomes make proteins by reading mRNA.

They are either free in the cytoplasm or attached to the rough ER. They are not membrane-bound.

general

U1 Q7 · 2 marksOrganelles

What is the role of mitochondria in a cell?

Tap to reveal answer
They make ATP (the energy currency) by aerobic respiration, so they are called the powerhouse.

Muscle cells have many mitochondria. They have their own DNA.

general

U1 Q8 · 2 marksOrganelles

What is the function of lysosomes?

Tap to reveal answer
Lysosomes digest worn-out organelles, bacteria and waste with enzymes.

They are called suicide bags because they can break the cell when it is damaged.

general

U1 Q9 · 2 marksTransport

Define osmosis.

Tap to reveal answer
Osmosis is the movement of water across a semi-permeable membrane from a lower to a higher solute concentration.

Water follows the solute. A red cell swells in pure water and shrinks in a strong salt solution.

general

U1 Q10 · 2 marksTransport

What does the sodium-potassium pump do?

Tap to reveal answer
It moves 3 sodium ions out and 2 potassium ions in for each ATP used.

It is active transport (against a gradient). It keeps the resting membrane potential of nerve and muscle cells.

general

U1 Q11 · 2 marksTissue

Define tissue.

Tap to reveal answer
A tissue is a group of similar cells with the same origin that do the same job.

Example: muscle tissue contracts; epithelial tissue covers.

general

U1 Q12 · 2 marksTissue

Name the four basic tissues of the body.

Tap to reveal answer
Epithelial, connective, muscular and nervous tissue.

Epithelial covers and lines; connective supports and binds; muscular moves; nervous carries signals.

general

U1 Q13 · 2 marksHomeostasis

Define homeostasis.

Tap to reveal answer
Homeostasis is keeping the internal environment steady (temperature, pH, glucose, water) despite outside changes.

It works through feedback loops.

general

U1 Q14 · 2 marksHomeostasis

Give one example of negative feedback.

Tap to reveal answer
Body temperature control: when it rises, sweating and skin vasodilation cool the body back to normal.

Negative feedback reverses a change. Other examples: blood glucose by insulin, blood pressure by baroreceptors. Positive feedback (childbirth, clotting) strengthens a change.

general

U1 Q15 · 2 marksEpithelium

Write any two functions of epithelial tissue.

Tap to reveal answer
Protection (skin) and absorption or secretion (gut lining, glands).

Also: filtration (kidney), sensation. Epithelium sits on a basement membrane and has no blood vessels.

U1 Q17 · 5 marksNucleus

Describe the structure and functions of the nucleus.

Tap to reveal answer

The nucleus is the control centre. Parts: nuclear envelope (two membranes with pores), nucleoplasm, chromatin (DNA with histone proteins) which condenses into chromosomes during division, and the nucleolus, which makes ribosomal RNA.

Functions: stores genetic information (DNA, 46 chromosomes in humans); controls protein synthesis by sending mRNA to ribosomes; controls cell division and growth; the nucleolus builds ribosome parts.

A mature red blood cell has no nucleus and cannot divide. added Your cell page notes that DNA, gene, chromatin and chromosome are the same material at different scales.

Marks: parts (3), functions (2). general

U1 Q18 · 5 marksOrganelles

Describe the mitochondrion: structure and functions.

Tap to reveal answer

Structure. A rod-shaped organelle with an outer smooth membrane and an inner membrane folded into cristae, enclosing the matrix. It has its own circular DNA and ribosomes.

Functions. Produces ATP by aerobic respiration (Krebs cycle in the matrix; electron transport on the cristae). Also heat production, calcium storage and a role in cell death (apoptosis).

Cells with a high energy demand, such as muscle, liver and sperm, have many mitochondria. Regular aerobic training increases their number.

Marks: structure (2), functions (2), example (1). general

U1 Q25 · 10 marksTransport

Explain the movement of substances across the cell membrane.

Tap to reveal answer

Introduction. The membrane is selectively permeable. Substances cross by passive transport (no energy), active transport (ATP) or bulk transport (vesicles).

APassive

  • Simple diffusion: small, non-polar molecules (O2, CO2) go from high to low concentration.
  • Facilitated diffusion: through channels or carriers (glucose, ions).
  • Osmosis: water through a semi-permeable membrane, towards the higher solute concentration. Isotonic, hypotonic and hypertonic solutions: a red cell stays the same, swells or shrinks.
  • Filtration: water and solutes pushed by pressure (kidney, capillary).

BActive

Pumps use ATP to move against the gradient. The sodium-potassium pump moves 3 Na out and 2 K in. It keeps cell volume and the nerve resting potential.

CBulk transport

Endocytosis brings things in (phagocytosis for particles, pinocytosis for fluid, receptor-mediated); exocytosis sends things out (hormones, neurotransmitters).

PassiveActiveBulk
EnergyNoYesYes
GradientWithAgainstEither
ExampleDiffusion, osmosisNa-K pumpPhagocytosis

Conclusion. Together they keep the cell’s inside different from the outside, which is the basis of homeostasis.

Marks: intro (1), passive (3), active (2), bulk (2), table and conclusion (2). general

U2 Q12 · 2 marksMuscle

U2 Q17 · 5 marksBone structure

Describe the structure of a long bone.

Tap to reveal answer

A long bone has:

  • Diaphysis: the shaft, made of compact bone, with a hollow medullary cavity (yellow marrow in adults).
  • Epiphyses: the two ends, made of spongy bone with red marrow, covered by articular cartilage.
  • Metaphysis: the growth region with the epiphyseal plate in children.
  • Periosteum: the outer fibrous covering (blood vessels, nerves, bone-forming cells).
  • Endosteum: lines the medullary cavity.

Microscopic: compact bone is made of osteons (Haversian systems): concentric lamellae around a central canal, with osteocytes in lacunae joined by canaliculi.

U2 Q20 · 5 marksMuscle contraction

Explain the sliding filament theory.

Tap to reveal answer

Contraction happens when thin actin filaments slide over thick myosin filaments, pulling the Z-lines closer, so the sarcomere shortens. The filaments themselves do not shorten.

  1. A nerve impulse reaches the neuromuscular junction; acetylcholine is released and a muscle action potential starts.
  2. Calcium is released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and binds troponin.
  3. Tropomyosin moves; myosin-binding sites on actin are exposed.
  4. Myosin heads (with ATP split into ADP and Pi) attach, forming cross-bridges, and pull (power stroke).
  5. New ATP detaches the head; the cycle repeats while calcium is present.
  6. Relaxation: acetylcholinesterase breaks acetylcholine, calcium is pumped back, tropomyosin covers the sites.

Marks: steps, about 1 mark each (six). general

U2 Q22 · 5 marksFatigue

What causes muscle fatigue and how can it be reduced?

Tap to reveal answer

Causes. Fatigue is the fall in force after prolonged use. Peripheral: lack of ATP and creatine phosphate, accumulation of lactate and hydrogen ions, loss of potassium and calcium handling, glycogen depletion. Central: reduced drive from the brain, with pain, boredom or poor sleep.

Not the same as soreness (DOMS), which comes 24 to 72 hours later from small tears.

Relief. Rest, slow breathing, hydration and food, warm-up and gradual loading, enough sleep, relaxation poses such as Shavasana.

Marks: causes (3), difference from soreness (1), remedies (1). general

U2 Q23 · 5 marksSkin

Explain the functions of the skin and its role in temperature regulation.

Tap to reveal answer

Functions. Protection (germs, UV, injury), sensation (touch, pain, temperature), temperature regulation, excretion (sweat), vitamin D synthesis, absorption, and water balance.

Temperature. When hot: skin vessels dilate (vasodilation), sweat glands secrete, and evaporation cools the body. When cold: vessels constrict, sweating stops, hair stands up and shivering starts. The hypothalamus is the control centre (negative feedback).

U2 Q26 · 10 marksMuscle

Describe the structure of skeletal muscle and explain muscle contraction with the energy used.

Tap to reveal answer

Introduction. Skeletal muscle makes up about 40% of body weight.

AStructure

Muscle → fascicles (bundles) → fibres → myofibrils → sarcomeres (actin and myosin). Layers: epimysium (whole muscle), perimysium (fascicle), endomysium (fibre). A fibre has a sarcolemma, sarcoplasm, many nuclei, a sarcoplasmic reticulum and many mitochondria. Attached to bone by tendons.

BContraction

Q25 · 10 marksPhysiology

Explain digestion in the stomach and the small intestine. Describe gastric juice and how acid is produced.

Tap to reveal answer

Introduction. Chemical digestion starts in the mouth (starch) and is mainly carried out in the stomach (protein) and small intestine (all food groups).

AIn the stomach

  1. Food becomes chyme by churning with gastric juice.
  2. Gastric juice has HCl, pepsinogen, mucus and intrinsic factor; gastrin (a hormone from G cells) stimulates secretion.
  3. Acid production: parietal cells pump hydrogen ions into the stomach lumen (a proton pump that uses ATP) and chloride follows, forming HCl. Inside the cell, carbon dioxide and water form carbonic acid (enzyme: carbonic anhydrase), which supplies the hydrogen ions. R&W
  4. HCl activates pepsinogen to pepsin, which breaks proteins into peptides at low pH.
  5. The stomach does not digest itself because of the mucus-bicarbonate barrier, inactive pepsinogen and rapid cell renewal.

BIn the small intestine

  1. Chyme enters the duodenum in small amounts; pancreatic juice (alkaline) and bile neutralise the acid.
  2. Carbohydrates: pancreatic amylase, then maltase, lactase and sucrase (lactase splits lactose into glucose and galactose).
  3. Proteins: trypsin, chymotrypsin and carboxypeptidase, then peptidases to amino acids.
  4. Fats: bile salts emulsify; lipase splits them to fatty acids and monoglycerides.
  5. The end products are absorbed through the villi.

Conclusion. The stomach prepares and starts protein digestion; the small intestine completes digestion and absorbs.

U4 Q26 · 10 marksGas exchange

Explain gas exchange, the transport of gases and the regulation of breathing.

Tap to reveal answer

Introduction. Gas exchange moves oxygen to cells and carbon dioxide away, and breathing is adjusted to match need.

AExchange

By diffusion across the respiratory membrane, driven by partial pressure gradients (alveolar pO2 104, venous 40; pCO2 40 and 45 mmHg).

BTransport

GasForms
Oxygen98.5% oxyhaemoglobin; 1.5% dissolved. S-shaped dissociation curve; Bohr effect
Carbon dioxide70% bicarbonate (carbonic anhydrase, chloride shift); 20–23% carbaminohaemoglobin; 7% dissolved

CRegulation

Medullary respiratory centre sets the rhythm; pons smooths it; chemoreceptors respond to CO2 (central) and O2 (carotid and aortic bodies); stretch receptors stop over-inflation; the cortex allows voluntary control.

Conclusion. The system stays balanced by negative feedback. Pranayama works on the voluntary override of this loop.

U4 Q27 · 10 marksYoga link

How does breathing affect the body in yoga practice? How do asanas and breath work together?

Tap to reveal answer

Introduction. Breath is the one automatic function that we can control, so it links the body and the mind in yoga.

AEffects of breathing

  • Respiratory: stronger diaphragm and intercostals, larger vital capacity, slower rate.
  • Nervous system: slow exhalation increases vagal tone and calms the body; fast breathing raises alertness.
  • Circulation: deep breathing aids venous return and lowers heart rate and blood pressure (practised with care).
  • Mind: attention on the breath reduces anxiety and improves focus.

BAsana and breath

PositionEffect on breathing
Forward bendsCompress the abdomen; lengthen the exhalation
Backward bendsOpen the chest; deepen inhalation
TwistsSqueeze and release the ribs; improve intercostal movement
InversionsChange lung blood flow; need care in hypertension
ShavasanaPassive recovery; natural slow breathing

CPranayama

Inhale (puraka), retain (kumbhaka) and exhale (rechaka). Examples: nadi shodhana, ujjayi, bhastrika, kapalabhati, bhramari.

Cautions. Avoid forced breath holding in hypertension, heart disease and pregnancy; learn under a teacher. Yoga can support people with asthma but does not replace treatment.

Conclusion. Breath awareness is the thread that joins posture and meditation.
